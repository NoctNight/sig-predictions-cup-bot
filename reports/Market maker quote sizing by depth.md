# Flatten the 50x ladder, weight the touch

Neither theory nor historical data supports a quote ladder whose size grows 50x from the touch to 8–10 ticks deep. The closed-form market-making models (Avellaneda–Stoikov, Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia, the Bergault–Guéant dealer ladders) never optimise size at all; when their size-indexed price ladders are inverted they imply a **flat density of roughly 1/b units per unit of price**, translated bodily by **b·q** with inventory, where b = √(γσ²e/(2AΔk)) is the ergodic skew slope. Empirically, fill intensity decays exponentially at **k ≈ 0.5–3.7 per tick** (steeper for large-tick books), more than **90% of executions happen at the best quote** in a tight book, and the only multi-level dataset with realised fills shows picking-off risk *rising* with depth so that net payoff per order falls from 1.94 to −0.02 across one to three ticks. The one ladder-shape sweep on real LOB replays (13 US large caps) found that only a touch-weighted Beta(1,2) profile had positive mean return, while flat and deep-weighted ladders lost; simulations with informed traders push 25–40% of volume one or two ticks deeper but never make a fixed deep ladder profitable. For a binary contract with a 0.005 tick the relative tick at p = 0.5 is 100 bp, far larger than any equity in these samples, so queue position and news sweeps, not distance decay, govern fills, and the DMM's 50x wall sits ahead of any deep order you post. Prediction-market variance scales as **p(1−p)/τ**, is roughly flat beyond a day from resolution and spikes in the final hours, so for 13 of the 14 days the half-spread is set by the tick and measured adverse selection rather than by diffusion. The recommendation is a **4–5-level ladder within ~7 ticks of the reservation price, flat-to-mildly-declining size per level (cumulative ≈3.5× the touch size, not 50×), total resting size capped by a worst-case-loss inventory limit, logit-space inventory skew, an OFI/queue-imbalance rule that pulls or shrinks the side about to be hit, and markout-gated per-level resizing**, with every quote's exposure time and every fill's 5-second and 60-second markout logged so A and k can be fitted from your own book.

## Theory optimises distance, never size, and implies a flat ladder

Every closed-form model in the Avellaneda–Stoikov family treats quote size as an exogenous constant and solves only for where the one bid and one ask should sit. Avellaneda and Stoikov's agent "is committed to respectively buy and sell one share of stock" at each quote, with the frozen-inventory reservation price **r = s − q·γσ²(T−t)** and a spread **γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)ln(1+γ/k)** that is independent of inventory because the arrival intensities are exponential ([AS 2008](https://www.math.nyu.edu/~avellane/HighFrequencyTrading.pdf)). Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia showed that this time dependence is an artefact of the expansion, that "the optimal quotes are almost independent of t, as soon as t is far from the terminal time T", and gave the ergodic closed forms: bid offset ≈ (1/γ)ln(1+γ/k) + ((2q+1)/2)·√(σ²γ/(2kA)·(1+γ/k)^{1+k/γ}), ask symmetric ([GLFT 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.3115)). Guéant's 2017 generalisation with trades of size Δ and a running inventory penalty (the Cartea–Jaimungal objective, which Feys shows is the second-order expansion of the CARA objective with φ = γσ²/2) collapses the whole family to one number: the skew slope **b = √(γσ²e/(2AΔk))**, with reservation mid S − b·q, half-width **c₀ = 1/k + bΔ/2**, spread affine in σ and skew linear in σ, and "replacing Λ by βΛ is equivalent to replacing σ² by σ²/β" ([Guéant 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.01862); [Feys 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01477)). Size enters only through Δ in the √Δ risk buffer.

Where size does become a decision variable is the dealer literature, and it does so indirectly: the price depends on the requested size. Bergault and Guéant's RFQ model sets the offset for a request of size z as δ(z) = δ*((w(q) − w(q+z))/z), a continuous nondecreasing function of the marginal value-function slope ([Bergault & Guéant 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01225)), and the closed-form approximations of Bergault, Evangelista, Guéant and Vieira make that argument **affine in both inventory q and size z**, i.e. the offset grows linearly in size with slope b/2 and the whole ladder translates with slope b ([BEGV](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04383)). Barzykin, Bergault and Guéant stream exactly such ladders to FX clients across sizes of 1–50 M€ and find quotes for larger sizes lying further from mid and the bid improving as inventory falls, with a "pure flow internalization" band inside which the dealer manages inventory by skew alone ([BBG FX](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02269)). Inverting a size-indexed price ladder into a size-at-each-distance ladder is a derivation the sources do not perform, but under the quadratic value-function proxy it is mechanical: posting one unit at the marginal reservation offset of each successive unit puts the n-th ask unit at c₀'' + b(−q + n − ½), so the cumulative size within distance D of mid is ⌊(D − c₀'')/b + q + ½⌋, a **flat ladder of 1/b units per unit price starting at a touch displaced by b·q**. Guilbaud and Pham reach the same structure from the other direction: with limit-order size l ∈ [0, l̄] as a control, the gain from spread capture is linear in l while the inventory-value change φ(y+l) − φ(y) is concave, so the optimal size is interior and, for quadratic φ, linear in (half-spread − b·q)/b ([Guilbaud & Pham 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.5040)).

The quadratic inventory-only proxy cannot generate any preference for depth; what can is adverse selection. GLFT's market-impact extension adds a constant half-width ξ/2 and multiplies the skew slope by e^{kξ/4}; a drift μ shifts both quotes by (μ/(γσ²))·b, equivalent to treating q_eff = q − μ/(γσ²) ([GLFT §5](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.3115)). Barzykin, Bergault, Guéant and Lemmel separate two informational channels: quote-insensitive adverse selection widens the spread through Δ_k terms, quote-sensitive (latency-arbitrage) adverse selection raises the concavity of the value function and hence the skew, and "price reading" by skew sniffers widens the zero-inventory spread but *reduces* the optimal skew shown, "particularly pronounced for top-of-book quotes" ([BBGL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20225)). The only model that allocates volume across levels endogenously is Cheridito and Weiss's reinforcement learner over three levels per side, and its allocation is state-contingent rather than a fixed shape: "volume is placed almost exclusively at the best bid and ask prices" with noise traders, split between the first and second level at 20 lots, and "distributed over all three price levels, with a clearly larger allocation to the third-best levels" once strategic informed traders enter ([Cheridito & Weiss 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18195)). The theoretical verdict is therefore unambiguous: size per level should be flat in distance at baseline, shifted bodily by inventory, and tilted deeper only in proportion to measured adverse selection at the touch. A 50x growth over eight ticks has no analytical basis.

## Fill intensity decays 40–98% per tick while adverse selection does not

The empirical fill curve is steep. On Nasdaq LOBSTER data for 2016, the exposure-time estimator Λ̂(δ) = fills/exposure-seconds fitted as log Λ̂ = log A − kδ gives **k̂ = 0.48/tick for AMZN (36.7-tick spread), 0.86 for NFLX, 1.58 for ORCL, 1.69 for MU and 3.72 for CSCO (1.1-tick spread)**, and k̂ ≈ 0.49–0.62 per tick on five FX pairs, so moving one tick deeper cuts fill intensity by about 40% in a small-tick book and by 98% in a large-tick one ([arXiv:2607.28323](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28323)). The Stockholm Stock Exchange lifetime-horizon numbers tell the same story in probabilities: buy limit orders filled **68% of the time at one tick, 33% at two, 12% at three** ([Hollifield, Miller & Sandås](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/hollifield-liquid2.pdf)). In LMAX EUR/USD, 99.9% of limit orders are cancelled, more than 90% of executions occur at the best, and "fill probabilities beyond one tick from the best quote are typically negligible" (event-level 2.0% at the best, 0.19% one tick deeper) ([Lokin & Yu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02572)). The mechanism is the queue-reactive one: market-order intensity at the first queue decays roughly exponentially in queue size and **no market orders arrive at the second queue while the first has orders**, which is why Poisson models "clearly overestimate" execution probabilities ([Huang, Lehalle & Rosenbaum](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563)). Median time-to-fill grows as distance^1.4 rather than the Brownian distance², because cancellations with power-law lifetimes truncate the race ([Eisler et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0701335)).

What makes the depth decision hard is that the market as a whole posts the opposite of the fill curve. Time-averaged depth is hump-shaped: on Schneider Electric in March 2011 the first level held **276 shares against 1,966 at level six**, a 7x rise, with limit-order arrival peaking at level two and vanishing by level 12–14 ([Abergel & Jedidi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1010.5136)); LMAX queues are smallest at the best and peak four to six ticks away; Paris Bourse books peak away from the best with power-law placement μ ≈ 0.6 ([Bouchaud, Mézard & Potters](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0203511)). The hump's location in ticks scales inversely with relative tick size, so for large-tick names liquidity sits at the touch and half of it is within ~2.5 levels, while for AMZN or GOOG the median liquidity distance is 47–49 ticks ([Jain et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08744)). Polymarket's book is nearly uniform: the median share of top-10 depth at level 1 is **0.136**, levels 4–10 each hold about 0.08, and the median KL divergence from uniform is 0.087 against 2.30 for a pure top-of-book book, with roughly 32 effective makers per market ([Dubach](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24366)). The 50x DMM ladder is a more extreme version of this backstop profile, and ABIDES's reference market maker is the same design (20 levels per side, half-spread spacing, equal size per level equal to 5% of recent traded volume) ([ABIDES](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/abides-sim/abides/master/agent/market_makers/AdaptiveMarketMakerAgent.py)); these are liquidity-provision backdrops, not optimised strategies, and no paper has published a sweep of their parameters.

Deeper is not safer per fill. At the back of the best queue in large-tick US names the adverse-selection cost was **0.53–0.89 ticks against a liquidity premium of 0.8 ticks**, leaving 0.03–0.15 ticks per order, while front-of-queue "touch value" was 0.21–0.31 ticks, so queue position was worth more than the residual edge ([Moallemi & Yuan](https://drupalgsb-test.paas.cc.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/25461/queue-value-2016.pdf)). On Binance BTC perpetuals, 1-second markouts were −0.06 bp at the front of a large queue but **−0.78 to −1.16 bp at the back**, the all-order average was −0.8 bp against a 0.5 bp rebate, and naive two-sided quoting lost about 60% in 3.3 days at an annualised Sharpe of −109 ([Albers et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18625)). On CME futures, naive passive posting produced adverse fills **81.5% of the time on ES, 65.8% on NQ, 82.9% on CL and 88.8% on ZN** ([Lalor & Swishchuk](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12721v1)), and ES passive fills give up 0.08–0.10 ticks of a 0.52-tick half-spread with the adverse move complete within one second and no reversion over 30 s ([arXiv:2609.18019](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.18019)). The only multi-level realised-fill table, Stockholm 1992, shows picking-off risk growing with depth (buys: 0.01, −0.09, −0.15 at one, two, three ticks) because deeper orders only fill after the price has moved through the touch, and the estimated payoff per order falls from **1.94 at one tick to 0.39 at two and −0.02 at three** ([Hollifield et al.](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/hollifield-liquid2.pdf)). The larger concession at depth is real, but conditional on execution it is roughly consumed by the move that triggered the fill.

For a 0.005-tick binary this evidence lands with extra force. At p = 0.5 the relative tick is 100 bp, fifty times CSCO's 1.94 bp, so the instrument is a large-tick book in the extreme: queue position and sweeps determine fills, deeper levels receive market orders only when shallower levels are exhausted, and k measured in ticks will be steep. The DMM's 50x deep wall compounds this. Under price-time priority an order you post at the same price as a level holding fifty times the touch size sits behind it, and it fills only when a news sweep exhausts that wall, which is precisely the adverse case. Your deep levels are therefore competitive only at prices where the DMM is thin (its touch and first one or two ticks) or at ticks between its rungs.

## Historical replays reward touch-weighted ladders; informed flow only rewards retreat

Three studies compare fixed volume shapes across a ladder, and together they bracket the answer. Jerome, Palmer and Savani swept scaled-Beta shapes over ten contiguous ticks on LOBSTER replays of 13 US large caps (March 2022, 60 one-hour episodes each). A ladder of ten ticks at any best-price offset had negative mean return (−0.24 to −2.58); among shapes, the **flat Beta(1,1) ladder returned −1.06 and was profitable on 0 of 13 tickers**, the touch-skewed **Beta(1,2) returned +0.58 (6/13 profitable) and was the only positive mean**, and the deep-skewed Beta(2,1) and Beta(5,1) returned −3.10 and −3.06; the authors state the profitable policies "skew the volume profile towards the best prices" ([Jerome, Palmer & Savani](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.03352)). Even the winner was low-Sharpe (mean 0.58 against a standard deviation of 16) and built large inventories, and an inventory-driven variant "suffered large losses in strongly trending periods." Wang et al. ran the same family in an agent-based market with 21 levels: with uninformed traders every ladder beat touch-only quoting (Beta(5,5) 4.82e6 vs 1.41e6), but once 13 of 25 agents were informed, **touch-only was the sole profitable policy (+7.95e6)**, every multi-level fixed shape lost 5e7–1e8 with deep Beta(5,1) losing least, and "adverse selection caused the MM's inventory to grow to 100 times the size it would have been without informed traders"; their conclusion is that the right response "may not be minimizing inventory but rather adapting swiftly by broadening its trading spread when risks arise" ([Wang et al. ICAIF 2024](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/97527/1/icaif24_29.pdf)).

Cheridito and Weiss's learned allocation gives the cleanest picture of how the tilt should move with toxicity. In their calibrated three-level simulator, filled volume came **99.7% from level 1 in a noise-only market (2 lots)**, 74.6/25.4/0.0% at 20 lots, and **61.8/29.9/8.3% across levels 1/2/3 with all three trader types**, while fill rates fell from 74.65% to 43.99% and cancel rates rose to 56%; the learned policy beat touch-only (TOP1) and one-tick-inside (TOP2) benchmarks in every market, e.g. 4.99 vs 0.73 and 2.05 normalised cash flow under informed flow ([Cheridito & Weiss](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18195v1)). Two negative results from that paper matter for sizing rules. The **inventory-skewed-size-at-the-touch benchmark (INV) was the worst policy under informed flow (−1.44)** because it "rebalanc[es] during adverse price moves", and a static |Q| penalty "is not effective in controlling inventory" in state-dependent markets. Size skew without a price skew and without a toxicity response rebalances you into the move.

Across the RL literature the recurring learned ladder is 60–100% of volume at the best price with the remainder one or two ticks deeper, the deeper share rising with informed flow, and inventory skew implemented as side-asymmetric size rather than a different depth ([Spooner et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.04216); [Niu et al. IMM](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.08918)). Multi-level agents beat single-level ones in the one head-to-head on real futures data (IMM 16.46 vs 4.36 EPnL on RB), but the gain was tied to queue-position retention rather than depth ([Niu et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2308.08918)). Practitioner frameworks offer no counter-evidence: Hummingbot's additive `order_level_amount` and Passivbot's multiplicative `double_down_factor` are the two increasing-with-depth schedules in wide use, Passivbot's being an explicit martingale capped by `wallet_exposure_limit`, and none of Hummingbot, Passivbot or Tribeca publishes a backtest comparing level counts or size schedules ([Hummingbot](https://hummingbot.org/strategies/v1-strategies/strategy-configs/order-levels/); [Passivbot](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/enarjord/passivbot/v7.5.0/docs/configuration.md)). For total posted size, Kühn and Muhle-Karbe's result is the cleanest closed form: the optimal inventory band is **±2·(half-spread)·(arrival rate)/(risk aversion·σ²)**, so the quantity you should be willing to hold scales with spread capture and fill rate and inversely with variance ([Kühn & Muhle-Karbe](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1309.5235)).

The synthesis is that the direction of the size-versus-depth tilt is not a design constant but a measured variable. With flow that is uninformed on your timescale, weight the touch and the first tick; as markouts at the touch deteriorate, move a quarter to a third of the volume one or two ticks deeper and widen; and never expect a fixed deep ladder to be profitable on its own, because every study that tried one lost.

## Pressure signals move fair value by up to half a spread and name the side to pull

Order-flow imbalance is linear and strong. Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov's OFI, the signed sum of best-quote size changes, explains an average **65% of 10-second mid-price variance across 50 S&P 500 stocks** against 32% for trade imbalance alone, with price impact β ≈ 0.45/AD where AD is half the average best-queue size, and a quadratic term adds only three points of R² ([CKS 2014](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1011.6402)). This yields a model-free fair-value shift in ticks, ≈ 0.45·OFI/AD, that an EWMA can accumulate online. Queue imbalance at the touch is the single best feature for large-tick instruments: the logistic slope of P(up | I) is **x₁ ≈ 2.0–2.7 for MSFT, INTC, CSCO, ORCL and MU (AUC 0.75–0.81)** versus 0.5–0.85 for GOOG, AMZN and TSLA (AUC 0.58–0.64), because with a one-tick spread price changes come from queue depletion ([Gould & Bonart](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1512.03492)). Cartea, Donnelly and Jaimungal measured the flow consequence directly: on INTC, buy market-order intensity was **0.024 in sell-heavy books, 0.048 neutral and 0.232 buy-heavy**, a tenfold range, with the post-trade mid biased in the imbalance direction, and between 91.6% and 99.9% of market orders filled entirely at the best price ([CDJ 2018](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:006addde-3a03-4d75-89c1-04b59026e1c0/files/me4008e0ecca779b45d59231ebca3e69c)). Stoikov's micro-price formalises the same information as the limit of expected future mids given (imbalance, spread), lives between bid and ask, and in the toy model equals M + I − ½ ([Stoikov](https://www.ma.imperial.ac.uk/~ajacquie/Gatheral60/Slides/Gatheral60%20-%20Stoikov.pdf)); Lipton, Pesavento and Sotiropoulos find the expected move until the next mid change is roughly linear in imbalance and reaches about a third of the spread in a highly imbalanced book, with the probability of an unfavourable move for a near-side resting order reaching almost 90% ([Lipton et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1312.0514)).

The control papers convert this into side-specific rules. CDJ's feedback rule posts a sell at the touch only if Δ/2 − ε⁺(J) + Φ⁺₁ > Φ⁺₀, i.e. the half-spread exceeds the expected post-market-order mid rise in the current imbalance state; in buy-heavy states "it is usually not optimal to post a sell order" and the side is withdrawn, producing no-trade regions on both heavy sides. Adding this imbalance conditioning lifted the annualised Sharpe of INTC touch quoting from **−7.5…−17 to 1.9…25.1** in their backtest, and the strategy stayed above the zero-intelligence benchmark even under a pessimistic back-of-queue fill model ([CDJ 2018](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:006addde-3a03-4d75-89c1-04b59026e1c0/files/me4008e0ecca779b45d59231ebca3e69c)). Pulido, Rosenbaum and Sfendourakis prove the size version: optimal ask volume is nonincreasing and bid volume nondecreasing in the efficient-price offset y = S − P, the maker may stop quoting a side entirely, and quoted sizes shrink with γ and σ and grow with market-order intensity ([Pulido et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.15599)). Lehalle and Mounjid show that for a resting buy the optimal policy under strongly negative imbalance is to cancel and wait, that "speed can be seen as a protection to adverse selection", and that institutional brokers' limit orders fill at an average absolute imbalance of 0.52–0.57 against 0.32–0.33 for high-frequency participants, a usable benchmark for how much adverse imbalance your own fills are accepting ([Lehalle & Mounjid](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1610.00261)). A slow maker that cannot cancel inside the imbalance's predictive window must substitute standing size asymmetry for cancellation.

Flow from your own fills carries a second skew. Cotton's OTC result is that with seller probability q and market width w, a flat maker should still shift its midpoint by **δ = (w/2)·log(q/(1−q)) ≈ 2w(q − ½)** and widen by γ ≈ 2w(q − ½)², so "skew is a flow phenomenon first and an inventory phenomenon second" and inventory skew enters as a linear term on top ([Cotton](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07690)). A regime-switching study makes the stakes explicit: a GLFT strategy's expected PnL **turns negative once P(buy) deviates about 0.08 from 0.50**, and a regime-aware agent recovers roughly 80% of the PnL that a stationary one loses ([arXiv:2609.11614](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.11614)). The own-fill statistics the literature supports are a Beta posterior on the buy fraction of recent fills (whose mean 2p̂ − 1 is the skew input), short-horizon markouts per fill, and a Bayesian informed-fraction estimate π̂ₙ = (a₀ + Σ1{Zᵢ > θ_c})/(a₀ + b₀ + n) whose adverse-selection cost enters the half-spread with MSE ≤ π(1−π)/n ([arXiv:2607.11888](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11888)). VPIN, with AR(1) ≈ 0.996 over a 50-bucket window and a 0.40 correlation with next-bucket absolute returns, is a slow symmetric regime signal for widening both sides, not a side-specific skew ([Easley, López de Prado & O'Hara](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/con_035928.pdf)). The practitioner rule that matches the evidence is to widen when the 1-second markout falls below −0.3 times the half-spread over a rolling window of several hundred fills, with widening and deepening preceding size cuts ([Quantvero](https://www.quantvero.com/?p=139)); Tribeca's `trds`/`/sec` rule, which stops one side after more than N one-sided fills in a window until they are made profitable, is the discrete version ([Tribeca wiki](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiki/michaelgrosner/tribeca/Home.md)).

## Binary contracts replace σ with p(1−p)/τ, which binds only in the last day

A binary's price is a probability, so its variance is structural. Feil and Nendel model p = f(L) with a latent belief diffusion and logistic f, giving **dp = p(1−p)·σ(t, logit p)·dW**, a running penalty on q²ς², and a terminal penalty **Φ = −γ_T·q²·p(1−p)**, the conditional variance of the settlement payoff; their numerics show spreads largest near p = ½ at any time, shrinking as liquidity rises toward settlement but widening again near settlement at p ≈ ½, and a skew that is nonzero even at q = 0 (positive for p < ½, negative for p > ½), decreasing in inventory and vanishing at the boundaries; against a myopic maximiser the optimal strategy cut PnL standard deviation 63% and 5% expected shortfall 76% at a 0.6% cost in mean ([Feil & Nendel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17991)). The empirical clock on Kalshi is Wright–Fisher: on 41,348 contracts the conditional variance **h² = [p(1−p)/τ + K·ν(V)·s²/4]·Δ** beats GARCH(1,1) by about 15% on interval score, realised volatility "peaks near 50 cents and falls toward the boundaries, and rises sharply in the final hours before resolution", and beyond about one day from resolution volatility is roughly flat so a pure 1/τ clock over-predicts the decline ([Xi, Moallemi, Pai & Wang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08199)). The AMM theory implies a liquidity schedule for the approach to resolution: holding a constant expected loss rate on the √(T−t) clock requires **L_t ∝ √((T−t)/T)**, and the depth profile that is loss-uniform under log-odds news is the constant-product shape 2√(p(1−p)), shallower in shares near 0.5 and deeper near the tails ([Moallemi, Robinson & Zhu](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.17428v1)).

With two weeks to resolution these facts sort the horizon into two regimes. Far from resolution the diffusion term is tiny: with the Feil–Nendel belief volatility σ_x = 0.6/yr^½, the one-hour price standard deviation at p = 0.5 is about **0.16 cents, a third of a 0.5-cent tick**, so the tick and adverse selection set the half-spread and inventory skew has to be anchored to realised rather than model variance; in the last day the deadline clock gives a one-hour standard deviation near **10 cents at p = 0.5**, sixty times larger, which is why spreads must widen and sizes shrink there (derived from the cited models). Jumps dominate the tails throughout: 47.8% of Kalshi Sports volume occurs in "large-move hours", the 2024 Trump contract moved about 10 points in 48 hours after the July 13 shooting and 11.5 points over one CME weekend closure, Polymarket's price changed in only 1.1% of 5-minute bins with very high excess kurtosis, and a single trader's four accounts, placing up to 71 orders a minute, were credited with part of a 50% to 61% run in thin liquidity ([Xi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08199); [Aktuğ & Torul](https://web.bogazici.edu.tr/torul/pridis.pdf); [Business Insider](https://businessinsider.nl/the-polymarket-whale-is-placing-as-many-as-71-bets-a-minute-in-favor-of-donald-trump/)). A diffusion-based inventory limit is therefore the wrong object; the binding limit is worst-case loss per share (p if long, 1−p if short) times the size a news sweep could fill, which is exactly the quantity a 50x deep ladder maximises.

Two more prediction-market regularities shape where in the price range to post. Makers do not escape the favourite–longshot bias: on Kalshi, average post-fee returns were **−9.64% for makers against −31.46% for takers**, but contracts at or below 10 cents lose more than 60% of invested money, makers buy 43.5% of 1–10 cent contracts, and makers' losses on those contracts remain significantly negative on five of six pre-close days ([Bürgi, Deng & Whelan](https://www.karlwhelan.com/Papers/Kalshi.pdf)). The bias is a function of time to resolution: the recalibration slope is 0.99 within an hour of resolution and 1.32 beyond a month (1.73–1.74 for politics and sports), so at two weeks a modest debiasing of the centre toward 0.5-away values is warranted and should decay with τ ([Le 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.19520v1)). Dalen's logit-space Avellaneda–Stoikov gives the operational form: displayed probability half-spread δ_p ≈ p(1−p)·δ_x so quotes compress at the extremes with a tick floor, an inventory cap ∝ 1/p(1−p), and a cross-event hedge ratio ≈ (S'ᵢ/S'ⱼ)·ρ shrunk by 0.5–1 ([Dalen](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15205v2)). Complements are the only true hedge and are frequently mispriced: 7,051 of 17,218 Polymarket conditions had YES+NO below $1 at a 5-cent threshold over a year, mostly in illiquid periods, while in liquid in-play NBA books single-market gaps lasted a median 3.6 seconds ([Saguillo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03474); [Cheng, Yang & Zou](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00864)). With no fees on your venue, the tick is the only floor on the half-spread; Polymarket's requirement of two-sided quotes when the mid is below 0.10 or above 0.90 and its one-third credit for one-sided liquidity are the venue-incentive constraints to check against ([Polymarket docs](https://docs.polymarket.com/developers/market-makers/liquidity-rewards)).

## Conclusion

The question "should size grow or shrink with depth" has a cleaner answer than the diversity of ladders in the wild suggests. Inventory risk alone says flat; every real-data and simulated test says the touch-weighted or flat ladder beats the deep-weighted one unless flow is informed, and when flow is informed the fix is to widen and shift a minority of volume one or two ticks deeper, not to stack size where a sweep will find it. A ladder that grows 50x over eight ticks is a liquidity backstop that writes a free option to whoever moves the price by eight ticks, and in a binary contract that mover is usually news. The practical consequence is that the depth tilt should be a live parameter driven by per-level markouts, the total posted size should be bounded by worst-case loss rather than by variance, and the ladder's main degree of freedom is where its reservation centre sits, which is governed first by order-flow pressure and second by inventory.

Two things the sources leave open deserve your own data. No published study tabulates realised markouts by distance beyond the best level for a modern electronic venue, and none fits A and k on a prediction-market book, so the first weeks of logged exposure time and markouts will be more informative than any parameter in this report. And because the DMM's wall changes queue dynamics at every price it occupies, your fill curve will not look like the exponential fits above; it will look like a touch regime governed by queue state plus a sweep regime governed by the DMM's exhaustion, and the logging below is designed to measure both.

## Recommended parameters for the SIG bot

**Notation.** m = current mid; m_adj = pressure-adjusted fair value; r = reservation price; h = half-spread in ticks (tick = 0.005); q = signed inventory in contracts; Q_cap = inventory cap on the relevant side; p = current mid as a probability; τ = time to resolution; S₀ = unit size. All distances are in ticks from r unless stated.

**Levels and offsets.** Five levels per side, placed at r ∓ (h + offset) with offsets **0, 1, 2, 4, 7 ticks**; drop level 5 entirely once τ < 24 h. Level 1 joins the best quote when the spread is at or below 2h + 1 and otherwise sits at r ∓ h; levels 2–5 are snapped to ticks the DMM does not occupy with more than 10× your level size (step one tick inward if it does), because an order behind a 50× wall fills only on sweep exhaustion. Hold resting orders rather than refresh them when nothing has changed; queue position is worth 0.1–0.2 ticks in large-tick books and more than the residual edge at the touch.

**Size multipliers per level (relative to S₀).** Size should not increase with depth. Baseline schedule is flat at the front and declining at the back; a single toxicity parameter θ ∈ [−1, +1] tilts it, interpolating linearly between the rows:

| θ (toxicity) | L1 (+0) | L2 (+1) | L3 (+2) | L4 (+4) | L5 (+7) | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| −1 benign (touch 5 s markout ≥ 0) | 1.50 | 1.00 | 0.50 | 0.25 | 0 | 3.25 |
| 0 neutral (default at launch) | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0.75 | 0.50 | 0.25 | 3.50 |
| +1 toxic (touch 5 s markout < −0.3h) | 0.50 | 0.75 | 1.00 | 0.75 | 0.50 | 3.50 |

Cumulative size seven ticks deep is about 3.5× the touch size, not 50×. Start at θ = 0 and let the markout rule below move θ in steps of 0.25.

**Unit size and inventory cap.** Q_cap,long = min(R_worst/p, R_var/σ_hold) and Q_cap,short = min(R_worst/(1−p), R_var/σ_hold), where R_worst is the dollar loss you accept on a wrong-way resolution or jump, R_var the one-sigma dollar loss you accept over a holding window, and σ_hold = √(p(1−p)·Δt_hold/τ) floored by trailing realised hourly volatility. Set S₀ on the accumulating side so the whole ladder cannot breach the cap: S₀,acc = max(0, Q_cap − |q|)/Σ(multipliers); on the reducing side S₀,red = S₀,acc·(1 + |q|/Q_cap). Post no bid when q ≥ Q_cap,long and no ask when q ≤ −Q_cap,short. Below p = 0.10, bid at level 1 only with 0.5× size (makers lose on sub-10-cent contracts), and keep both sides posted if the venue's rewards require it. From τ = 24 h, multiply all sizes by √(τ/24 h).

**Half-spread.** h = max(1 tick, 1/k + b·S₀/2 + α̂ + w_flow), where 1/k and b come from the fitted intensity (below), α̂ is the EW mean adverse markout at the touch in ticks (the Bayesian π̂·α_info estimate once ≥ 50 fills exist), and w_flow = 2h·(f_b − ½)² is Cotton's second-order flow widening. While k is uncalibrated use h = max(1 tick, 1 tick + α̂). Add 1 tick when |p − 0.5| < 0.1 and τ < 24 h; add √(24 h/τ) − 1 ticks (capped at 4) inside the final six hours.

**Inventory-skew formula.** r = m_adj − b·q with **b = √(γ·σ²·e/(2·A·S₀·k))** in price units, σ² the per-second variance estimate used above (trailing realised, floored by p(1−p)/τ), A and k the fitted intensity parameters. Until A and k are calibrated use the fallback **b = 2h/Q_cap**, so that at half the cap the near-side quote sits at the old mid and at the cap that side disappears. Compute the skew in logit space when p < 0.15 or p > 0.85: r_x = logit(m_adj) − b_x·q with b_x = b/(p(1−p)), then r = logistic(r_x), so the skew fades automatically at the extremes. Double b when τ < 24 h (terminal penalty γ_T q² p(1−p)). Never run size skew without this price skew: size-only skew was the worst policy under informed flow.

**Pressure and imbalance skew rule.** Three layers, in order:
1. Fair-value shift: m_adj = m + clip(0.45·OFI_w/AD, −h, +h) ticks, with OFI accumulated over a window of roughly 3–5 average inter-trade times (start at 30 s) and AD = half the average best-queue size; replace with the fitted micro-price g(I, S) once you have a week of transitions.
2. Side rule: let I = (Q_bid − Q_ask)/(Q_bid + Q_ask) at the touch (positive = buy-heavy) and ε⁺ = expected post-buy-MO mid move from your logged data (initialise as tick·(2·P(up|I) − 1) with P(up|I) = 1/(1 + e^{−2.5·I})). If ε⁺ > h, pull the ask level 1 to the level-2 price (keep levels 2–5); symmetric for bids on ε⁻. If |I| > 1/3 scale level-1 and level-2 sizes on the side about to be hit by clamp(1 − |I|, 0.25, 1) and on the other side by clamp(1 + |I|, 1, 1.5).
3. Own-fill flow: f_b = Beta-posterior mean of the buy fraction over an EW window of the last ~30 fills (prior Beta(5,5)); shift r by −2h·(f_b − ½) and widen by w_flow. If more than 3 same-side fills occur within 60 s with negative cumulative 5 s markout, pause that side's level 1 for 60 s or until the inventory is offset.

**Markout-based resizing rule.** For every (side, level) keep EW means of the 5 s and 60 s markouts (half-life 100 fills; act only after 30 fills) and define edge_i = concession_i − markout_i(60 s), where concession_i is the signed distance from the mid to the fill price at fill time. If edge_i < 0, halve that level's multiplier (floor 0.25× the schedule) and, if i = 1, add 1 tick to h. If edge_i > 0.5 tick for two consecutive 100-fill windows, raise the multiplier by 25% back toward the schedule. Globally, when the touch 5 s markout over the last 200–500 fills falls below −0.3h, move θ up by 0.25 (toward toxic) and add 1 tick to h; move θ back down by 0.25 when it recovers above −0.1h for a full window. Treat a 1 h realised variance above 2× its trailing 24 h median as a VPIN-style regime signal: multiply all sizes by 0.5 and h by 1.5 until it normalises.

**Requote triggers.** Re-price the ladder when any of the following fires; otherwise leave resting orders untouched to keep priority:

| Trigger | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Mid or m_adj moves | ≥ 1 tick |
| Reservation price r moves (inventory change, OFI) | ≥ 0.5 tick |
| Fill at any level | immediately re-skew; replace the filled level after 5 s if its edge is positive, 60 s if negative |
| Touch imbalance I crosses | ±1/3 (side rule engages or releases) |
| Pressure window | re-evaluate every 1–5 s; do not use 60–300 s practitioner delays |
| Volatility regime | 1 h realised variance > 2× trailing median |
| Time to resolution | taper begins at τ = 24 h; recompute h and sizes every 15 min thereafter |
| Order age | no age-based refresh; cancel only on the above or on a book-state change that drops level 1 priority behind more than 10× your size |

**What to log to calibrate A and k from your own fills.** Per resting order: order id, side, level index, price, distance from mid in ticks and in units of the current spread, size, queue volume ahead at placement, DMM size at that price, spread in ticks, I, OFI_w, p, τ, timestamps for placement, each modification, cancellation and each fill, and accumulated exposure seconds per (distance bin, spread state). Per fill: fill price, size, mid at fill, mid at +1 s, +5 s, +60 s and +10 min, book state (I, queue sizes) at fill, resulting inventory, and whether the fill came from a single sweep that crossed more than one of your levels. Per observed market order on the tape: side, size, levels swept and whether your quote at distance δₙ was reached (the Bernoulli outcome Yₙ for the online MLE of k). Estimate Λ̂(δ) = N_fill(δ)/T_exp(δ) per distance bin with exposure summed over days before dividing, fit log Λ̂ = log A − kδ on bins at least one tick off the touch, model the touch bin separately as a Beta posterior per (near-queue, far-queue) bucket, and rescale A so the model's two-sided fill rate at the zero-inventory depth matches the empirical one. Expect |k̂ − k| to shrink as N^{−½}, so 100 fills give roughly ±10% on k; refit weekly, and fit in spread units as well as ticks because the spread-unit k transfers across price levels and across markets.
